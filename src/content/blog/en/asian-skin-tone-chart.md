## Asian Skin Tone Chart: Why One Label Isn't Enough

**Quick answer:** Asian skin tones vary on two separate lines: depth (light, medium, deep) and undertone (cool, neutral, warm or olive). The chart below gives 12 reference shades with names and hex codes. Find your depth first, then your undertone, and don't assume yellow-looking skin is warm. It can also be neutral or olive.

Searches like "Asian skin tone," "Asian skin color" and "Asian skin colour" usually come from a practical problem. You want to know which shade or color family you belong to, and the answers you find are either too vague ("fair, medium, dark") or too narrow ("Asian skin is yellow-toned").

Both fail for the same reason. Asia includes East, Southeast and South Asia, among other regions, and skin tones across it run from very fair to deep brown, with every undertone in between. A single label can't hold that range, and neither can a chart that tries to sort people into boxes. What a chart can do is give you a shared vocabulary: a grid where you find your own depth and undertone and learn what to call them.

That is what the chart below is. It is a reference grid for describing skin, not a classification of people, and your result says something about your skin, not your background.

## Depth vs Undertone: Two Different Questions

Most confusion comes from mixing up two traits that are independent of each other:

- **Depth** is how light or dark your skin is overall. It is what you notice first, and what words like light, medium and deep describe.
- **Undertone** is the color beneath the surface: cool (pink, rosy or bluish), warm (yellow, peachy or golden), neutral (a balance of both) or olive (a muted gray-green or khaki cast).

Two people can share the same depth and have different undertones, and the reverse is just as common. Someone with light, cool skin and someone with light, warm skin are both "fair," yet a pink lip color and a golden jacket look very different on each of them.

Olive gets its own column in the chart because it is the one most often missed. It can sit on top of any base undertone, but it behaves differently enough to need its own description.

Depth and undertone are also two of the four traits behind a [12-season color analysis](/color-analysis). The other two are contrast (how different your skin, hair and eyes are from each other) and chroma (how soft or vivid your coloring is). The chart covers only the first two, so it narrows things down without giving you a full season.

## The Asian Skin Tone Chart: 12 Reference Shades

Read the chart in two steps. Pick the depth table closest to your overall skin, then pick the row whose description sounds most like your undertone.

The names are descriptive labels made for this chart, not brand shade names. The hex codes are illustrative swatches, not measurements of real skin or of any product. Screens, lighting and printing change how every color looks, so use the codes to compare relative differences (lighter or darker, pinker, yellower or greener), not to match makeup.

Real skin sits between these swatches. If you fall between two depths, go with the one that matches your jawline and inner arm rather than your cheeks. If you fall between two undertones, that usually points to neutral or olive, which the sections below explain.

## How to Find Your Depth

Depth is the easier of the two traits, and most people land within one step of the right answer. A few habits make it more reliable.

1. **Use soft daylight.** Stand facing a window or in open shade, with no filters and a bare face. Warm bulbs and screens change how deep your skin looks. Our guide to [how lighting affects your colors](/blog/how-lighting-affects-your-colors) shows how much.
2. **Look at the jawline and inner forearm, not just the cheeks.** Cheeks flush, tan and carry makeup. The inner arm and the skin just under the jaw show your base color more honestly.
3. **Separate tan from depth.** A tan darkens your surface color for a while, but it does not change your undertone. If you've had a lot of sun, compare against your inner arm and note it.
4. **Pick the nearest table, not a perfect match.** The chart has three depths on purpose. If two look right, choose the one closer to your neck.

## How to Find Your Undertone

Undertone is harder to see, so use more than one test and trust the answer that keeps repeating. Work in daylight with a bare face.

- **White paper test:** Hold a plain white sheet next to your face. Skin that looks yellow or golden against it points warm. Pink or rosy points cool. If it looks balanced, think neutral. If it looks dull, gray or khaki, think olive.
- **Gold versus silver:** Hold a gold item and a silver item against your jaw. Whichever makes your skin look healthier points to your undertone. If both look fine, you may be neutral. If both look slightly sallow, or rose gold clearly wins, that is a common sign of olive.
- **Two-color drape:** Hold a coral or peach cloth under your chin, then a raspberry or rose one. Choose the one that makes your face look clearer and less shadowed.

The wrist-vein test is popular, but it is hard to read on medium and deep skin and on olive skin, so don't let it overrule the others. For a guided version, try our free [skin undertone test](/tools/undertone-test), or read [how to determine your skin undertone](/blog/how-to-determine-skin-undertone) for the full walkthrough.

If your tests contradict each other, that is information rather than a failure. Mixed results are typical of neutral and olive undertones, which is why those two rows are the ones people most often move between.

## Why Yellow Skin Isn't Always Warm (and Other Mix-Ups)

Most wrong answers come from a handful of predictable mistakes.

| What you notice | Why it misleads | What to check instead |
|---|---|---|
| "My skin looks yellow" | Surface yellowness is common and shows up across undertones, so it doesn't prove warm. | Gold versus silver: if gold looks sallow on you, you probably aren't warm. |
| "I flush pink easily" | Pink redness can sit on top of a neutral or olive base and make you read cool. | Look at calm, bare skin along the jaw, not after exercise or in the cold. |
| "I have dark hair and eyes" | Hair and eye color affect contrast, not undertone. | Judge the skin on its own. |
| "I tan easily" | A tan changes depth for a while, not undertone. | Compare with your inner arm. |
| "Foundation looks orange or gray on me" | A classic sign of the wrong undertone family, or of a missed olive cast. | Try shades labeled neutral or olive and test along the jaw. |
| "I look greener in winter, yellower in summer" | An olive cast changes how visible it is with the season and your tan. | Retest in the same light on two different days. |

Olive deserves a closer look because it is the undertone most often confused with warm or neutral. It appears in people of every background and at every depth, which is covered in our guide to [olive skin by ethnicity](/blog/olive-skin-by-ethnicity). ## From Shade to Season: What the Chart Doesn't Cover

Your chart result tells you your depth and undertone, which is roughly half of what a season needs. A full season also depends on contrast and chroma, so two people in the same cell of the chart can land in different seasons.

Seasonal analysis is well established in East Asia, where it is known as 퍼스널컬러 in Korea and パーソナルカラー in Japan. Our guide to [Korean personal color analysis](/blog/korean-personal-color-analysis) explains how those systems work and how they compare with the 12-season approach.

## Key Takeaways

- **Asian skin has two separate dimensions.** Depth (light, medium, deep) and undertone (cool, neutral, warm, olive) are independent, and a single label hides both.
- **Yellow-looking skin isn't automatically warm.** Check with the white paper test and gold versus silver before deciding.
- **Neutral and olive are easy to miss.** Mixed or contradictory test results often point to one of them.
- **The hex codes are illustrative.** Use them to compare relative differences, not to match makeup or paint.
- **A chart is a starting point.** Season also depends on contrast and chroma, so confirm your result by trying colors near your face in daylight.

Once you can name your depth and undertone, shopping for foundation and colors stops being guesswork. Start with the two steps above, repeat them on a second day, and go with the answer that holds.
